# Daily TOEIC Journey — Course Constitutional Specification

## 1. Authority and scope

This document is the stable constitutional specification for the course. All curriculum maps, lesson plans, lessons, exercises, assessments, reviews, audio assets, learner-state updates, and AI-assisted generation processes must conform to it. When a downstream artifact conflicts with this document, this document governs unless it is deliberately revised and the dependent artifacts are revalidated.

This specification defines permanent principles and constraints. It does not define daily lesson order, learner-specific state, or generation prompts.

## 2. Course vision

Daily TOEIC Journey is a mastery-gated self-study system for a Vietnamese learner beginning at zero, Pre-A1, or a very weak beginner level. It develops general English through approximately CEFR C1 while progressively building the listening, reading, speed, and reasoning required for high performance on TOEIC Listening & Reading.

The course is not a test-trick database. Its governing relationship is:

> General English competence supplies the language; TOEIC preparation teaches the learner to apply that competence accurately and efficiently under a particular test format.

Early stages prioritize language foundations, sound recognition, basic comprehension, and learning habits. TOEIC contexts appear gently and purposefully. Later stages increase authentic workplace language, paraphrase density, inference, distractor quality, passage complexity, and time pressure. At every stage, useful real-world English and assessment performance should reinforce one another.

The course has no fixed number of days. It may contain hundreds of lessons, reviews, and remediation units. Advancement is determined by demonstrated readiness, not calendar time or lesson count.

## 3. Default learner profile

The default learner:

- speaks Vietnamese as a first language;
- begins with zero, Pre-A1, or fragile English knowledge;
- studies independently and may not have teacher support;
- needs Vietnamese explanations that remain understandable without prior grammar terminology;
- benefits from English examples being preserved in English, with translation when it serves a clear learning purpose;
- needs gradual progression, repeated encounters, detailed feedback, and explicit connections;
- may forget vocabulary and grammar quickly;
- may translate word by word and transfer Vietnamese word order into English;
- may struggle to hear English sounds, final consonants, clusters, stress, reductions, and connected speech;
- may have limited vocabulary and little knowledge of grammatical labels;
- aims for strong general competence and potentially high TOEIC Listening & Reading performance, with a long-term target around CEFR C1.

Common Vietnamese-learner tendencies are teaching hypotheses, not stereotypes. Lessons should address them when evidence or the target concept makes them relevant, while allowing individual variation.

## 4. Course outcomes

By completing the full learning journey and meeting its mastery criteria, a learner should be able to:

1. comprehend and analyze English from basic words and sentences through complex workplace, general, and selected academic texts;
2. understand spoken English from careful beginner speech through varied, connected, relatively fast speech across common TOEIC-relevant accents;
3. recognize and use the grammar, vocabulary, discourse relationships, and sentence structures needed for broad B2/C1 comprehension;
4. read notices, messages, emails, forms, schedules, advertisements, articles, and multi-document sets efficiently and critically;
5. recognize paraphrases, implications, speaker intentions, reference relationships, and cross-document connections;
6. apply robust strategies to TOEIC Listening & Reading Parts 1–7 without depending on shortcuts that replace language knowledge;
7. function in common real-world situations involving daily life, travel, services, workplace communication, meetings, schedules, requests, clarifications, announcements, and email;
8. diagnose important errors, select suitable review actions, and increasingly manage independent learning;
9. distinguish what is known receptively, what is understood deeply, and what can be used actively;
10. continue improving beyond the course without permanent dependence on Vietnamese translation.

The course supports high TOEIC performance but does not guarantee CEFR certification, a particular TOEIC score, or achievement within a fixed period.

## 5. CEFR and TOEIC: related but not equivalent

CEFR describes broad communicative language proficiency across reception, production, interaction, and mediation. TOEIC Listening & Reading measures performance on a specific receptive-skills assessment in particular communicative and workplace-oriented contexts. A TOEIC score is therefore not a complete statement of CEFR ability, and CEFR level does not mechanically determine a TOEIC score.

The course may use approximate TOEIC score orientations to:

- sequence question difficulty;
- choose plausible performance milestones;
- calibrate speed and test-format exposure;
- communicate the direction of travel.

Such bands are planning guidelines, not conversions, promises, entry requirements, or exact equivalences. Individual profiles can be uneven: a learner may have B2-like reading but B1 listening, or strong grammar knowledge but weak timed performance. Phase decisions must use skill evidence rather than a single score alone.

## 6. Governing learning cycle

Every substantial concept follows this cycle:

**Learn → Understand → Observe examples → Analyze → Practice with guidance → Practice independently → Mix with previous knowledge → Apply in real and TOEIC contexts → Receive detailed feedback → Identify mistakes → Review through spaced repetition → Demonstrate mastery → Progress**

Presentation is not mastery. A concept is properly taught only after the learner has had proportionate opportunities to:

- understand its meaning and purpose;
- recognize it in multiple forms;
- distinguish it from related or confusing concepts;
- use or apply it when productive practice is appropriate;
- make and analyze representative mistakes;
- encounter it in meaningful contexts;
- retrieve it again after time has passed;
- apply it without topic labels or excessive hints;
- meet the relevant mastery criteria.

The detailed operational rules for this cycle are defined in `LEARNING_SYSTEM.md`.

## 7. Knowledge tracks

The tracks below are coordinated strands, not independent courses. Curriculum planners must identify dependencies and deliberate connections among them.

### 7.1 English foundations

- the alphabet and essential written conventions where needed;
- sound–letter awareness without implying perfectly regular spelling;
- word, phrase, clause, and sentence;
- subject, verb, object, complement, and modifier, introduced in learner-appropriate language;
- basic sentence patterns and English word order;
- parts of speech as functional tools rather than labels to memorize;
- capitalization and punctuation;
- statements, questions, commands, and negation;
- dictionary, transcript, IPA, and exercise notation taught before independent use.

### 7.2 Grammar

The grammar strand develops dependency-aware control of:

- nouns, countability, number, pronouns, articles, and determiners;
- adjectives, adverbs, comparison, and modification;
- lexical verbs, `be`, `have`, auxiliaries, verb forms, agreement, tense, and aspect;
- questions, negation, imperatives, and modal meanings;
- objects, complements, infinitives, gerunds, and participles;
- prepositions, conjunctions, coordination, and subordination;
- passive voice;
- relative, noun, and adverb clauses;
- conditionals and reported speech;
- reduced clauses and participial modification;
- advanced sentence structure, information packaging, reference, cohesion, and discourse relationships needed for B2/C1 reading and listening.

The authoritative high-level order is in `KNOWLEDGE_DEPENDENCIES.md`. A common structure appearing in TOEIC material is not a reason to bypass its prerequisites.

### 7.3 Vocabulary and lexical competence

Vocabulary is selected through frequency, usefulness, coverage, prerequisite value, learner need, and domain relevance—not as random daily lists. It progresses through:

1. survival and high-frequency function words;
2. daily life, people, places, time, numbers, actions, and descriptions;
3. common communication and service situations;
4. workplace and business vocabulary;
5. broad TOEIC-relevant domains;
6. abstract, cross-domain, and selected academic/general vocabulary supporting B2/C1 comprehension.

Required TOEIC domain coverage includes office work, meetings, schedules, recruitment, human resources, training, purchasing, contracts, finance, banking, sales, marketing, retail, customer service, manufacturing, logistics, shipping, travel, airports, hotels, restaurants, events, healthcare, technology, construction, real estate, insurance, maintenance, facilities, announcements, and company policies.

The depth of a lexical entry increases with level. When pedagogically appropriate it includes:

- headword or expression and Vietnamese meaning;
- learner-appropriate English definition;
- pronunciation and IPA where useful;
- part of speech and relevant grammar behavior;
- natural example and context;
- collocations and common word partners;
- useful synonyms, antonyms, word family, and register;
- common confusions and Vietnamese-learner risks;
- TOEIC usage and common paraphrases.

Vocabulary status must distinguish at least: first exposure, recognized with support, recognized independently, understood in context, retrievable after delay, usable in controlled production, and durable. Not every TOEIC word needs equal productive mastery; planners must state the intended depth.

### 7.4 Pronunciation and listening perception

Pronunciation is a core comprehension track, not an appendix or accent-elimination program. It develops:

- sound awareness and useful phonemes;
- vowels and consonants that commonly challenge Vietnamese learners;
- final consonants, grammatical endings, and consonant clusters;
- syllables and word stress;
- sentence stress, rhythm, focus, and thought groups;
- linking, assimilation where useful, reductions, weak forms, and contractions;
- common transformations between careful written forms and connected speech;
- intelligible production when production helps perception;
- recognition of multiple standard accents encountered in TOEIC-style listening.

The main aim is reliable word and meaning recognition in continuous speech. Accent imitation is optional and secondary to intelligibility and comprehension.

### 7.5 Listening

Listening grows through the sequence:

**sound → word → phrase → sentence → question–response → short exchange → conversation → announcement/talk → multi-speaker comprehension → paraphrase and inference → high-speed natural speech**

The learner develops sound discrimination, keyword and boundary recognition, gist, specific information, speaker role, location, intention, numbers, dates, times, names, spelling, prediction, connected-speech recognition, paraphrase recognition, inference, distractor detection, selective note-taking, dictation, and optional shadowing. TOEIC Parts 1–4 are introduced progressively after their underlying skills.

### 7.6 Reading

Reading grows through the sequence:

**word → phrase → sentence → short paragraph → notice/message → email/advertisement/schedule/form → article/business document → longer passage → double passage → triple passage → advanced inference and cross-document reasoning**

The learner develops structural decoding, subject–verb identification, vocabulary in context, reference tracking, context clues, skimming, scanning, evidence location, main idea, detail comprehension, inference, purpose, tone, paraphrase recognition, multi-source synthesis, speed, and time management. TOEIC Parts 5–7 are layered onto these abilities rather than introduced at full difficulty.

### 7.7 Paraphrasing and meaning equivalence

Paraphrase recognition is a course-wide strand. It advances from direct synonym pairs (`purchase` → `buy`) through phrase substitutions (`submit an application` → `apply`), grammatical transformations, active/passive equivalents, time and quantity restatements, cause/effect relations (`due to` → `because of`), business equivalents, indirect statements, implication, and cross-sentence reformulation.

Learners must learn to verify preserved meaning, participants, time, polarity, degree, and conditions. Similar words are not automatically equivalent in every context.

### 7.8 Real-world communication and learner autonomy

Although TOEIC L&R is receptive, selected speaking and writing activities support memory, grammar, pronunciation, and real-world use. The course includes practical communication, dictionary and transcript use, self-explanation, error logging, study planning, and responsible strategy selection. Productive work supports—rather than displaces—the main listening and reading objectives.

## 8. Language policy

### 8.1 Stable rules

- Core explanations for beginners are in clear Vietnamese.
- English terms are preserved when useful and immediately explained in Vietnamese at first required use.
- English examples remain in English. Vietnamese translations are supplied when they clarify meaning or prevent false understanding.
- Translation is a scaffold, not the final comprehension process.
- Instructions must not require English knowledge beyond the learner's established level unless a Vietnamese explanation is available.
- Vietnamese and English must be visually distinguishable in examples and answer explanations.
- Natural meaning takes priority over literal word-by-word correspondence.

### 8.2 Gradual change in support

- **Pre-A1–A1:** Vietnamese-led explanation; short English classroom language is glossed; translations are frequent but purposeful.
- **A2:** Vietnamese remains primary for new grammar and complex instructions; familiar English definitions and predictable instructions increase.
- **B1:** bilingual support becomes selective; learners first attempt direct comprehension before consulting translations.
- **B1+/B2:** English definitions, summaries, and strategy explanations increase; Vietnamese is retained for subtle contrasts, difficult errors, and efficient remediation.
- **B2+/C1:** English is the normal medium for much input and analysis; Vietnamese remains available where it adds precision or resolves persistent transfer problems.

Vietnamese support is never removed abruptly. Learner evidence may justify slower or faster fading.

## 9. No unexplained knowledge

No lesson may depend silently on an untaught term, rule, notation, strategy, vocabulary item, audio convention, question format, or assumed cultural knowledge.

If potentially new knowledge is necessary, the lesson must do exactly one of the following:

1. explain it at the depth required now;
2. cite the prerequisite concept or lesson where it was established and provide a brief retrieval cue;
3. label it **Future concept** or **Optional extension**, give enough orientation to avoid confusion, and state that mastery is not required.

Terms such as *auxiliary verb*, *clause*, *participle*, *gerund*, *infinitive*, *modifier*, *transitive verb*, *relative pronoun*, and *subordinate clause* may not be used as if self-explanatory. A familiar plain-language explanation should precede or accompany technical terminology.

Glossing every incidental word is not always required. However, unknown incidental language must not prevent completion of the target task; it must be inferable, glossed, simplified, or deliberately taught.

## 10. No artificial lesson-length limit

There is no fixed word limit, page limit, token target, exercise maximum, or uniform lesson template length. A lesson is as long as necessary to teach, practice, assess, and connect its objective properly.

The governing rule is:

> Completeness over brevity; relevance over volume.

Planners limit new core knowledge and intrinsic complexity—not useful explanations, examples, feedback, or varied practice. Long content must be navigable, chunked, and purposeful. A short topic may require a short lesson; a foundational distinction may require extensive explanation and practice. A demanding lesson may be split into coherent units when fatigue would prevent learning, but it must not be shortened merely for appearance or generation convenience.

## 11. Learn Mode and TOEIC Mode

### 11.1 Learn Mode

Learn Mode optimizes understanding and correction. It permits clear teaching, Vietnamese support, examples, guided steps, hints, repeated audio, unlimited reasonable time, transcript access after an honest first attempt, and detailed analysis. Errors are learning evidence rather than penalties.

### 11.2 TOEIC Mode

TOEIC Mode measures independent performance under declared conditions. It uses realistic formats and distractors, controlled audio and transcript access, no hints during scored work, exam pacing, and separate post-attempt explanations. Scored answers must not be exposed before submission.

### 11.3 Relationship

Mode must be labeled. Difficulty and mode are separate: a beginner can complete a short, simple TOEIC-mode set, and an advanced learner can return to Learn Mode for a difficult concept. The proportion of TOEIC Mode increases with readiness, but Learn Mode remains available for new or weak knowledge at every phase.

## 12. Content and assessment quality rules

All artifacts must satisfy these permanent rules:

1. Accuracy takes priority over novelty or creativity.
2. Explain before testing unless the activity is explicitly diagnostic.
3. State and verify prerequisites; do not assume them.
4. Do not simplify a rule until it becomes false or misleading.
5. Teach high-value exceptions at the appropriate time; label later exceptions without overloading beginners.
6. Separate beginner-friendly mental models from technical linguistic detail.
7. Use natural, contextually plausible English.
8. Use respectful, varied, internationally intelligible people and situations.
9. TOEIC-style material must resemble plausible workplace, business, travel, and service communication without copying protected test content.
10. Avoid ambiguity in scored questions; every scored item must have a defensible keyed answer.
11. Distractors must be plausible yet clearly wrong given the stated language and context.
12. Do not present test heuristics as universal grammar or guaranteed shortcuts.
13. Avoid jargon that does not improve understanding.
14. Preserve continuity and explicitly connect prerequisites, contrasts, future concepts, and applications.
15. Prefer adequate depth to superficial coverage.
16. Revisit important concepts through retrieval, spacing, and varied contexts.
17. Distinguish recognition, comprehension, recall, controlled production, and independent application.
18. Distinguish language knowledge from exam strategy and report weaknesses separately.
19. Do not advance solely because a date or lesson number has passed.
20. Use mastery and dependency evidence to guide progression.
21. Keep the target construct clear: an item should not accidentally depend on several untaught skills.
22. Ensure instructions and examples are accessible at the current level.
23. Make audio scripts natural for speech, not written prose read aloud without adaptation.
24. Treat IPA as a tool taught gradually, never as an unexplained prerequisite.
25. Use translations that reflect contextual meaning, not misleading word substitution.
26. Verify facts, numerical data, answer keys, internal references, and transcript–audio agreement.
27. Separate diagnostic, instructional, practice, and scored assessment results.
28. Make accessibility possible through clear headings, consistent notation, transcripts at the correct stage, and text alternatives for essential visual information.
29. Track revisions so state and curriculum do not silently diverge.
30. Never invent learner performance data.

## 13. Answer explanation standard

For instructional and post-assessment review, a correct letter or word alone is insufficient. A complete explanation includes, when relevant:

- the correct answer and reconstructed complete sentence or meaning;
- why it is correct;
- the governing grammar or vocabulary principle in accessible language;
- the exact contextual, textual, or audio evidence;
- the reasoning path a learner can reuse;
- why each plausible alternative is wrong;
- the trap represented by each distractor;
- the clue to notice next time;
- a short corrective example or contrast when useful;
- the error category and recommended review target for important mistakes.

Explanations must address the actual item, not paste a generic rule. In TOEIC Mode, explanations appear after the scored attempt. In listening work, the explanation should distinguish a language gap, sound-recognition gap, memory/attention issue, paraphrase failure, inference error, distractor choice, and time-pressure error where evidence permits.

## 14. Vietnamese-learner support

Content designers should proactively consider, when relevant:

- articles and countability;
- plural endings and third-person singular `-s`;
- tense and aspect marking;
- English subject requirements and word order;
- adjective/noun/adverb form confusion;
- pronoun reference;
- preposition choice;
- translation-shaped sentence construction;
- final consonants, consonant clusters, and grammatical endings;
- difficult sound distinctions;
- word and sentence stress;
- reduced and linked speech.

Support should use contrast, perception practice, articulatory guidance, visual marking, minimal or near-minimal pairs where useful, and meaningful contexts. It must never claim that all Vietnamese learners share the same problem or that one Vietnamese dialect predicts all difficulties.

## 15. Course-wide progression constraints

- New material must fit the dependency architecture or explicitly include remediation for missing prerequisites.
- Core knowledge, useful extensions, and future concepts must be labeled by role.
- New core load must be manageable for the learner's phase and current state.
- Old knowledge must be retrieved and interleaved after initial isolation.
- Pronunciation must support listening; grammar and vocabulary must support both listening and reading.
- Paraphrasing begins simply and grows throughout the course.
- TOEIC exposure proceeds from context and micro-skills to isolated items, mixed sets, timed sections, and full simulations.
- Full exam difficulty and strong time pressure must not dominate beginner learning.
- Weak prerequisites receive remediation before advanced learning relies heavily on them.
- Minor errors do not automatically block all progress; targeted review may accompany advancement.
- Assessments use multiple evidence types and report skill profiles, not only total percentages.
- C1-oriented study must include nuanced general English, complex discourse, precision, and independent comprehension—not only TOEIC optimization.

## 16. Future lesson specification classes

Future lesson standards must define flexible lesson families rather than one mechanical template.

### 16.1 Universal components

Every lesson must include:

- identity, phase, mode, and estimated study conditions rather than a hard time promise;
- purpose and observable learning objectives;
- prerequisites and links to established concepts;
- distinction among new core knowledge, review knowledge, and extensions;
- accessible instruction or an explicit diagnostic label;
- meaningful examples or input;
- learner action, not presentation alone;
- feedback or a route to detailed solutions;
- a mastery check appropriate to the objective;
- review/state-update instructions and the next knowledge connection.

### 16.2 Conditional components

Use when pedagogically relevant: quick diagnostic, prior review, formal rule, mental model, formula, comparison, common traps, Vietnamese-learner note, translation, real-world application, TOEIC application, challenge, homework, flashcards, mistake-log prompt, and one-minute summary.

### 16.3 Skill-specific components

- **Grammar:** form, meaning, use, contrasts, constraints, examples, error analysis, and varied application.
- **Vocabulary:** intended depth, pronunciation, meaning, context, collocation, word family or paraphrase as level-appropriate, and retrieval.
- **Pronunciation:** perception before or alongside production, articulation guidance where useful, audio references, discrimination, and connected-speech transfer.
- **Listening:** pre-listening purpose without revealing answers, first listen, controlled repeats, questions, delayed transcript, transcript/audio analysis, and optional dictation or shadowing.
- **Reading:** purpose, staged text complexity, evidence-based questions, structural or discourse analysis, and strategy appropriate to text type.
- **TOEIC:** part and subskill tags, realistic conditions, distractor rationale, pacing goal, and separation of test attempt from teaching review.

### 16.4 Assessment-specific components

Scored assessments require a construct map, coverage table, conditions, scoring rule, answer key, evidence-based explanations, skill-level interpretation, mastery/remediation decision, and state-update instructions. They must not teach new required knowledge inside a scored item unless that item is explicitly unscored experimentation.

## 17. Governance and change control

Stable architecture documents change only through intentional review. A proposed change must identify:

- the educational reason;
- affected phases, dependencies, standards, and state schemas;
- migration needs for existing curriculum or lessons;
- compatibility risks;
- validation performed.

Learner state, review queues, lesson outputs, and operational schedules are mutable and must not be copied into this document. `ROADMAP.md` owns phase outcomes; `LEARNING_SYSTEM.md` owns operational learning rules; `KNOWLEDGE_DEPENDENCIES.md` owns high-level prerequisite order; `REPOSITORY_ARCHITECTURE.md` owns file responsibilities and pipeline contracts.

## 18. Constitutional completion test

A downstream plan is acceptable only if it can answer yes to all of the following:

- Can a true beginner understand the instructions and prerequisites?
- Does it build real language ability as well as TOEIC performance?
- Are pronunciation and listening perception connected?
- Are reading and grammar complexity staged?
- Are vocabulary, paraphrasing, review, error learning, and remediation systematic?
- Can explanations be as complete as learning requires?
- Does progression depend on mastery rather than elapsed time?
- Can the system preserve continuity through files and state rather than conversation memory?
- Is every scored answer defensible and reviewable?
- Does advanced study remain broad enough to support C1-oriented competence?

# Daily TOEIC Journey — Operational Learning System

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how teaching, practice, review, assessment, remediation, and progression operate. `COURSE_SPEC.md` supplies constitutional rules; `ROADMAP.md` supplies phase outcomes; `KNOWLEDGE_DEPENDENCIES.md` supplies prerequisite order. Future curriculum and lesson systems must implement the processes below and record their decisions in repository state rather than relying on conversation memory.

## 2. The mastery loop

Every important concept travels through a repeatable loop:

1. **Diagnose** — Check prerequisite readiness and existing knowledge without treating an untaught item as failure.
2. **Learn** — Present meaning, purpose, and a learner-appropriate mental model.
3. **Understand** — Check the learner can explain or distinguish the concept, not merely repeat wording.
4. **Observe** — Show natural examples from simple to more varied contexts.
5. **Analyze** — Identify form, meaning, clues, constraints, and relationships.
6. **Practice with guidance** — Use prompts, models, highlighting, partial steps, or immediate feedback.
7. **Practice independently** — Remove topic-revealing support and require a complete decision.
8. **Interleave and apply** — Mix the concept with old knowledge and apply it in real-world and, when ready, TOEIC contexts.
9. **Receive explanatory feedback** — Explain the keyed answer, evidence, reasoning, alternatives, traps, and next-step clue.
10. **Classify errors** — Record root cause with calibrated confidence rather than only right/wrong.
11. **Space and retrieve** — Revisit after increasing intervals in changed contexts.
12. **Demonstrate mastery** — Show accuracy, explanation, independence, transfer, and durability appropriate to the skill.
13. **Progress or remediate** — Advance, continue with targeted review, or repair the prerequisite.

The loop is recursive. A failed transfer task may return the learner to analysis, a different explanation, or an earlier prerequisite. A mastered concept remains in low-frequency maintenance.

## 3. Concept records and learning status

Each planned concept should have a stable identifier and a curriculum record containing:

- name and plain-language meaning;
- track and phase;
- prerequisites;
- intended depth: awareness, recognition, comprehension, controlled use, independent use, or automatic application;
- core examples and contrasts;
- known Vietnamese-learner risks where relevant;
- real-world and TOEIC applications;
- assessment evidence types;
- review behavior;
- current status in learner state.

Recommended learner-status scale:

| Status | Evidence |
|---|---|
| `unseen` | No instruction or reliable evidence |
| `introduced` | Explanation/examples encountered |
| `supported` | Can respond with guidance |
| `independent` | Can apply in isolated work without hints |
| `interleaved` | Can select it when mixed with alternatives |
| `durable` | Can retrieve and transfer it after spacing |
| `maintenance` | Stable; sampled at longer intervals |
| `remediation` | Important misunderstanding or prerequisite weakness requires repair |

Statuses are skill-specific. A word may be `durable` for reading recognition but only `supported` for pronunciation or productive use.

## 4. Prerequisite gate

Before teaching a concept that depends on earlier knowledge:

1. list the essential prerequisites;
2. retrieve a small representative sample;
3. distinguish a temporary lapse from conceptual weakness;
4. choose one of three actions:
   - proceed normally;
   - proceed with a just-in-time refresher and schedule review;
   - pause the dependent concept and remediate the blocking prerequisite.

Not every prerequisite needs perfect recall. A prerequisite blocks progress when misunderstanding would make the new mental model unreliable, cause repeated failure, or make explanations depend on unknown knowledge.

## 5. Knowledge roles and cognitive load

Every lesson labels knowledge as:

- **Core knowledge:** required now and included in mastery decisions.
- **Review knowledge:** previously taught and intentionally retrieved or interleaved.
- **Useful extension:** deepens understanding but is not required for current progression.
- **Future concept:** gives brief orientation only; full teaching and testing come later.
- **Incidental language:** needed only to understand the context and glossed or made inferable.

### Cognitive-load principles

- Limit new core relationships, not explanation length or useful practice.
- Count difficulty across vocabulary, grammar, instructions, modality, memory, speed, and task novelty.
- Keep non-target language below the level at which it masks the target construct.
- Use chunking, headings, worked examples, and progressive disclosure for long lessons.
- Change only a few difficulty dimensions at once during instruction.
- Split a lesson when fatigue or interference would prevent retrieval, while preserving the complete learning sequence across the resulting units.
- Do not force identical grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation, reading, listening, and TOEIC quotas into every lesson.
- Review-focused, assessment-focused, pronunciation-focused, and remediation lessons are legitimate.
- There is no arbitrary word, page, token, exercise-count, or study-time maximum.

## 6. Explanation and example sequence

For a new concept, the preferred sequence is:

1. communicative need or concrete situation;
2. intuitive Vietnamese explanation;
3. simple natural examples;
4. meaning check;
5. form/pattern and necessary terminology;
6. annotated examples and non-examples;
7. comparison with the nearest known alternative;
8. controlled variation across person, time, polarity, or context as relevant;
9. important exception at the appropriate depth;
10. real-world and TOEIC connection.

Examples should form a purposeful set. They should vary one property at a time early, then combine properties later. Counterexamples must clarify the boundary rather than confuse a beginner.

## 7. Exercise progression

Exercise design follows a flexible ladder. Important concepts may require several passes at a level; simple concepts may move quickly.

| Level | Purpose | Typical formats | Support |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recognition | Notice form, sound, or meaning | matching, identify, select example, sound discrimination | high |
| Guided practice | Apply one step with a model | scaffolded choice, highlighted clue, partial sentence, guided replay | substantial |
| Controlled practice | Apply a constrained rule | fill-in, transformation, sentence ordering, focused dictation | moderate |
| Independent practice | Choose and execute without topic hints | mixed items, short answer, sentence building, unmarked listening/reading | low |
| Mixed practice | Discriminate among old and new knowledge | cumulative MCQ, error correction, mixed tense/lexis, varied audio | none during attempt |
| Contextual application | Use meaning in a realistic situation | mini-dialogue, email, schedule, workplace scenario, translation with purpose | authentic context |
| TOEIC application | Apply under test-like format | Parts 1–7 items/sets | mode-dependent |
| Challenge/transfer | Generalize to unfamiliar or dense contexts | inference, cross-document task, what-if transformation, explanation | none during attempt |

Available formats include multiple choice, fill-in, matching, ordering, error identification/correction, transformation, sentence building, English-to-Vietnamese comprehension, purposeful Vietnamese-to-English production, short answer, contextual vocabulary, listening comprehension, dictation, paraphrase matching, inference, reading comprehension, mini-dialogues, and workplace tasks.

Quantity is determined by coverage needs. Variation should test distinct features, confusions, contexts, and retrieval routes. Many nearly identical items create fluency illusions and are not a substitute for varied evidence.

## 8. Exercise construction and answer explanations

### Item requirements

Every scored item must declare or make recoverable:

- target concept/subskill;
- prerequisites;
- mode and timing condition;
- keyed answer;
- evidence and rationale;
- distractor rationale for selected-response items;
- difficulty drivers;
- error categories the response may indicate.

Avoid accidental ambiguity, culturally dependent guesses, and distractors that are wrong only because the writer intended them to be. Listening questions must agree exactly with the final audio and transcript. Reading evidence must be locatable or a clearly justified inference.

### Solution requirements

After an instructional attempt, explain as relevant:

1. the correct answer in full context;
2. the rule, meaning, or evidence;
3. a reusable reasoning path;
4. why each alternative fails;
5. the trap type;
6. the clue to notice next time;
7. a contrast or corrected example;
8. the likely error category and appropriate review.

For listening, quote or point to the decisive short transcript segment after the attempt and connect heard form to written form. For reading, identify exact evidence and explain any inference bridge. A generic explanation that does not resolve the actual alternatives is incomplete.

## 9. Learn Mode

Learn Mode exists to change competence.

### Conditions

- Teaching precedes non-diagnostic testing.
- Vietnamese scaffolding, hints, worked examples, and unlimited reasonable study time are allowed.
- Listening begins with an honest transcript-free attempt when feasible, followed by controlled repeats.
- Transcript reveal occurs after initial comprehension effort, then supports sound–text mapping and analysis.
- Learners may pause, compare examples, consult glossaries, and retry after feedback.
- Scores are formative and must not be mixed with TOEIC-mode estimates.

### Default listening protocol

1. Preview the purpose and necessary context without revealing answers.
2. First listen without transcript.
3. Record gist, confidence, and any recognized details.
4. Listen again for targeted information.
5. Answer and explain evidence where possible.
6. Reveal transcript only after the defined attempt.
7. Compare expected written form with the actual sound; mark linking, reductions, stress, and missed boundaries.
8. Analyze paraphrases and distractors.
9. Use optional short dictation or shadowing if it addresses the diagnosed cause.
10. Re-listen later without transcript.

Shadowing is not automatic busywork. It is used when repeating rhythm or sound chunks helps perception and intelligibility.

## 10. TOEIC Mode

TOEIC Mode exists to measure and train test performance.

### Conditions

- Declare part, time limit, replay policy, allowed materials, and transcript policy before the attempt.
- Give no hints or answer-revealing feedback during scored work.
- Use realistic but original item structures and plausible distractors.
- Preserve standardized conditions when comparing results.
- Release answers, transcripts, and full explanations only after submission.
- Record accuracy, timing, confidence, omissions, and error categories.

### Progressive use

1. format demonstration, unscored;
2. simple isolated item, untimed;
3. small homogeneous set;
4. mixed untimed set;
5. short timed cluster;
6. timed part or section;
7. realistic full simulation;
8. targeted re-testing with a parallel form.

Learn Mode and TOEIC Mode can occur in one lesson only if their boundaries and scores are explicit. A TOEIC-mode miss should normally be reviewed in Learn Mode.

## 11. Spaced repetition system

### Default schedule

For new or repaired knowledge, schedule retrieval approximately at:

- **R0:** later in the same lesson or session;
- **R1:** next lesson;
- **R2:** several days later, commonly 3–4 days;
- **R3:** about one week after learning;
- **R4:** about two weeks after learning;
- **R5:** about one month after learning;
- **maintenance:** progressively longer intervals based on evidence.

These are anchors, not rigid deadlines. Performance modifies the interval:

- accurate, confident, independent transfer may expand it;
- correct but slow/uncertain retrieval keeps or slightly shortens it;
- a lapse prompts quick re-retrieval;
- conceptual misunderstanding returns to explanation/remediation rather than merely scheduling more identical flashcards;
- high-importance prerequisites receive more conservative spacing.

### What is spaced

- vocabulary meaning, form, pronunciation, collocation, and paraphrase at the intended depth;
- grammar form–meaning contrasts and sentence parsing;
- sound distinctions, reductions, and boundary patterns;
- listening and reading strategies in new texts;
- TOEIC trap recognition without teaching superficial tricks;
- recurring personal errors.

### Variation requirement

Review changes context, speaker, modality, item form, or direction of retrieval. A word first matched to Vietnamese may later be retrieved from an English definition, heard in connected speech, chosen by collocation, and recognized as a paraphrase. Identical repetition is reserved for limited cases such as initial sound discrimination or deliberate fluency measurement.

### Review queue priority

A suggested priority function considers:

`importance × weakness × recurrence × prerequisite impact × due-ness`

with a diversity constraint so a single weak category does not consume every session. The actual formula belongs to implementation standards; state must preserve the factors and reason.

## 12. Interleaving

Interleaving begins after a concept has received enough isolated practice for the learner to understand the choice. Mixing too early can obscure the initial rule; mixing too late creates cue dependency.

### Progression

- **Stage A — blocked:** one new contrast with high support.
- **Stage B — near contrast:** new concept versus the most confusable known alternative.
- **Stage C — local mix:** new concept plus several recent concepts in the same track.
- **Stage D — cross-track mix:** grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation/listening, and reading clues interact.
- **Stage E — unlabeled context:** the learner is not told which concept is tested.
- **Stage F — timed/transfer:** mixed selection under realistic conditions or unfamiliar context.

For example, Present Simple is isolated first, contrasted with Present Continuous next, later mixed with Past Simple, agreement, frequency adverbs, time prepositions, workplace vocabulary, and listening reductions. Success must not depend on the page title revealing the answer.

Interleaving ratios depend on novelty and performance. A new concept may dominate early practice, but its share decreases as it stabilizes and older due items re-enter.

## 13. Error-driven learning

### Error taxonomy

Record the most plausible root cause rather than only the surface item:

- concept misunderstanding;
- missing or fragile prerequisite;
- forgotten vocabulary;
- grammar confusion;
- collocation/word-form confusion;
- careless execution or answer transfer;
- listening sound misrecognition;
- connected-speech or boundary failure;
- pronunciation-related listening error;
- memory/attention overload;
- paraphrase failure;
- inference or evidence-location failure;
- distractor attraction;
- strategy misuse;
- time-pressure error;
- ambiguous/defective item.

The taxonomy may be refined in standards, but historical records must remain interpretable.

### Error record

An important error entry should contain:

- date, lesson/item, concept, skill, and mode;
- learner answer and keyed answer;
- evidence and confidence;
- suspected cause, with confidence in the diagnosis;
- correction in the learner's own words when possible;
- immediate repair completed;
- future review date/type;
- recurrence count and related pattern;
- status: open, improving, resolved, or monitoring.

Do not record every typo forever. Promote an error to a pattern when it recurs, affects an important prerequisite, reveals a misconception, or creates high TOEIC cost.

### Error-to-action mapping

| Cause | Primary response |
|---|---|
| Concept misunderstanding | alternative explanation, worked contrast, guided practice |
| Missing prerequisite | revisit prerequisite before dependency |
| Forgotten vocabulary | retrieval with context, collocation, and adjusted spacing |
| Listening misrecognition | sound–text mapping, discrimination, slowed diagnostic audio then normal speed |
| Paraphrase failure | explicit equivalence analysis and varied paraphrase network |
| Inference failure | locate facts, state inference bridge, reject unsupported leaps |
| Careless error | checklist or attention routine; avoid unnecessary reteaching |
| Time pressure | compare untimed/timed evidence, automate bottleneck, adjust pacing |
| Defective item | exclude from mastery evidence and repair the item |

## 14. Review architecture

### Within-lesson review

Retrieve prerequisite knowledge, revisit the new concept after intervening activity, and finish with a concise mastery check and review instruction.

### Next-lesson review

Use a small retrieval sample before new dependencies. Do not reteach the entire previous lesson unless evidence warrants it.

### Weekly or learning-cycle review

Mix recent content with selected older due content across tracks. Include at least one task without topic labels and one explanation or evidence task.

### Periodic cumulative review

Sample durable knowledge across broader intervals, new contexts, and integrated skills. Include personal error patterns and check for forgetting that immediate lesson scores concealed.

### Phase review

Confirm prerequisite network, skill profile, independence, transfer, and readiness for the next phase. Separate instructional review from scored re-assessment.

Review is not a duplicate lesson. It changes retrieval cues, contexts, modalities, and contrasts.

## 15. Remediation system

Remediation is proportional and diagnostic.

### Remediation ladder

1. Give precise corrective feedback and one immediate retry.
2. Present a clearer or alternative explanation.
3. Reduce linguistic/task complexity while preserving the target.
4. Contrast the target with the learner's wrong model.
5. Revisit a missing prerequisite.
6. Provide additional guided and controlled practice.
7. Assign a short focused remediation lesson.
8. Reassess with new items after a useful interval.

Use the smallest intervention that repairs the cause. A minor lapse may need only retrieval; repeated conceptual failure may require a prerequisite branch. Remediation items must not simply repeat the original wording.

### Progress during remediation

- A severe blocking gap pauses concepts that directly depend on it.
- Unrelated tracks may continue if cognitive load remains reasonable.
- Partial mastery may accompany advancement with an explicit review plan.
- The learner is not trapped in endless repetition for low-impact errors.
- Persistent difficulty prompts a new diagnosis, not infinite use of the same method.

## 16. Knowledge connections

Every core concept record and substantial lesson should make relevant links explicit:

- **prerequisite:** what makes today's concept possible;
- **related:** a concept sharing form, meaning, or use;
- **contrast/confusion:** the closest alternative and decisive difference;
- **future:** what this concept will later support;
- **real-world:** where the learner encounters it;
- **TOEIC:** which parts, item types, or comprehension processes use it;
- **Vietnamese transfer:** a relevant contrast, only where useful.

Connections are tested, not merely displayed. Learners should sometimes explain a link, sort examples, transform one form into another, or locate the connection in a text/audio.

## 17. Paraphrase learning system

Paraphrasing progresses through:

1. high-frequency synonyms and transparent phrase substitutions;
2. word-family and part-of-speech transformations;
3. active/passive and clause/phrase transformations;
4. time, quantity, cause, result, purpose, and condition restatements;
5. business collocations and indirect speech acts;
6. implication, stance, and multi-sentence reformulation.

Each paraphrase task checks invariants: participant, action/state, time, polarity, quantity/degree, cause, condition, and certainty. Learners must also identify near-paraphrases that change one important feature.

Paraphrase records should link vocabulary, grammar, listening, reading, and TOEIC applications so the same relationship can reappear in different modalities.

## 18. Assessment layers

### Lesson-level checks

- quick prerequisite diagnostic;
- observation of guided/independent practice;
- meaning or knowledge questions;
- self-explanation;
- mini quiz;
- transfer-oriented mastery check.

### Weekly or learning-cycle review

Mix recently learned and older due knowledge. Use enough items for useful diagnosis but avoid a false precision unsupported by sample size.

### Periodic cumulative assessment

Measure retention, discrimination among related concepts, integration, and application in new contexts.

### Phase assessment

Measure exit capabilities and next-phase prerequisites by track. Include language competence and phase-appropriate TOEIC evidence.

### TOEIC-mode assessment

Progress from isolated items to timed sets, sections, and full simulations. Standardize conditions and keep post-test analysis separate.

### Evidence dimensions

Do not use one percentage for every decision. Interpret:

- **accuracy:** correct outcome;
- **understanding:** can explain meaning/rule/evidence;
- **independence:** support required;
- **fluency:** time/effort appropriate to phase;
- **transfer:** success in changed context or format;
- **durability:** retrieval after delay;
- **confidence calibration:** whether confidence matches accuracy.

### Default mastery interpretation

No universal numeric threshold governs every skill. Curriculum standards may set calibrated thresholds by construct, but the conceptual rule is:

- **Strong:** high accuracy plus sound explanation, independence, delayed retrieval, and transfer.
- **Partial:** central meaning is understood; errors are localized or fluency is developing.
- **Weak:** repeated misconception, heavy support dependence, or failure of a necessary prerequisite.

High-stakes progression decisions require multiple evidence points. One lucky multiple-choice result is insufficient; one careless miss does not erase established mastery.

## 19. Progression algorithm

After each lesson or review cycle:

1. validate the assessment items and conditions;
2. update concept and skill evidence;
3. classify important errors;
4. schedule spaced retrieval;
5. identify blocking versus non-blocking gaps;
6. update the review queue and next-lesson constraints;
7. choose the next action:
   - advance to a dependent concept;
   - continue same-level varied practice;
   - interleave for discrimination;
   - remediate;
   - assess durability;
8. record the reason.

Calendar order may suggest what becomes available; it does not force advancement. A generated next lesson must consult current state and prerequisite evidence.

## 20. Future lesson architecture

### Universal requirements

Every lesson includes identity/phase/mode, objectives, prerequisites, knowledge roles, accessible input/explanation, learner practice, feedback path, mastery evidence, review/state instructions, and connection forward.

### Conditional components

Use as needed: diagnostic, review, mental model, formal rule, annotated examples, comparison, extension, Vietnamese-learner note, real-world scenario, TOEIC transfer, error correction, challenge, flashcards, homework, summary, and mistake-log prompt.

### Lesson-family emphasis

- A grammar lesson emphasizes form–meaning–use, contrast, sentence analysis, and transfer.
- A vocabulary lesson emphasizes intended lexical depth, contexts, collocations, retrieval, and paraphrase.
- A pronunciation lesson emphasizes perception, intelligibility, audio evidence, and listening transfer.
- A listening lesson follows transcript-control and sound-analysis protocols.
- A reading lesson emphasizes evidence, structure, discourse, and text-type strategies.
- A review lesson maximizes retrieval and discrimination while minimizing new core knowledge.
- A remediation lesson targets a diagnosed cause and ends with a parallel reassessment.
- An assessment lesson standardizes conditions and does not conceal teaching inside scored sections.

No lesson is forced to contain every possible section.

## 21. State-update contract

After a final lesson, the state updater records only evidence that actually occurred:

- concepts introduced/practiced/assessed and resulting statuses;
- vocabulary by skill and depth;
- grammar/foundation status;
- listening, reading, pronunciation, paraphrase, and TOEIC subskill evidence;
- important errors and pattern changes;
- due review items and reasons;
- lesson completion conditions and assessment mode;
- unresolved prerequisites and next-lesson constraints.

It must not infer mastery from content exposure, overwrite history without trace, or copy entire lesson explanations into state. State is concise operational memory; lessons remain the evidence artifacts.

## 22. Operational quality checklist

Before a learning unit is finalized, verify:

- prerequisites are established or explained;
- new core load is reasonable;
- English and Vietnamese support match the phase;
- examples are natural and answers defensible;
- practice progresses beyond recognition;
- old knowledge is retrieved at appropriate spacing;
- explanations address wrong options and evidence;
- listening transcript access follows the declared mode;
- real-world and TOEIC applications are proportional to phase;
- review and state updates are specified;
- failure has a remediation route;
- no fixed length or calendar rule has displaced learning needs.
